Within-dataset performance is strong for tree-based models. Cross-dataset generalization collapses — consistently, and for identifiable reasons. This page walks through both.

03 · Why

Four models, four different reasons for their score

A score alone doesn't explain itself. Here's the mechanism behind each one, including a test that ruled out the wrong explanation.

Random ForestSTRONG

Splits are chosen by information gain over labeled data — the model is directly optimizing for the boundary between benign and malicious. With explicit regularization (max_depth=12, min_samples_split=10) it beat even the Milestone 2 baseline (AUROC 0.94 vs. 0.88 on CAM‑LDS).

Its FPR (10–11% on CAM‑LDS) reflects the 5% cap being honestly unreachable in some folds — the cap is a target, not a guarantee, once applied to genuinely unseen test data.

XGBoostSTRONG

Same paradigm as Random Forest — label-guided splits — with sequential boosting that targets the residual cases a single tree ensemble misses. Performance is essentially tied with RF, as expected for two members of the same supervised-tree family.

Needed a fix mid-project: without an explicit thread cap, it oversubscribed CPU cores under concurrent SLURM jobs and stalled at 2–3 of 15 repeats. Pinning n_jobs=4 to match the actual allocation recovered full throughput.

1D‑CNNUNSTABLE

Mean performance is respectable, but Casino's F1 standard deviation (±0.369) is enormous. Root cause: CasinoLimit is 99.6% duplicated at the feature-vector level (only 356 unique patterns across 91k rows). The CNN's inner validation split occasionally lands on an unrepresentative slice of those few unique patterns, miscalibrating its decision threshold for that run.

A degenerate-split guard (retry until both classes have ≥5 examples in validation) reduced — but did not eliminate — the collapse. This is a real, diagnosed failure mode, not unexplained noise.

Isolation ForestPARADIGM MISMATCH

On CAM‑LDS, AUROC sits at 0.555 — barely above chance. Isolation Forest assumes anomalies are a sparse minority in a dense normal population. CAM‑LDS is 54% attack — there is no minority to isolate.

Ruled out an alternative explanation: we tested fitting the model only on confirmed-benign rows ("novelty mode"), hypothesizing that a mixed training population was diluting its idea of "normal." AUROC didn't move (0.532 → 0.555, within noise). The failure is the random-split isolation mechanism itself, not what it's trained on — confirmed by the same model scoring 0.821 AUROC on Casino, where attack really is a 17% minority.

04 · The Real Finding

Cross-dataset transfer: train once, test on the other environment

Every model above was evaluated on the environment it was trained for. 05 · The Asymmetry

Why one direction is worse than the other

Both directions fail, but not the same way — and the difference is explainable from what each dataset actually contains.

CAM-LDS → Casino
Actively inverted

RF and Isolation Forest reach AUROC ≈ 0.02, with tiny standard deviation — a stable, reproducible failure. CAM‑LDS's 19 techniques and 54% attack rate teach patterns specific to that environment; applied to Casino, those patterns actively point the wrong way. This is the signature of overfitting to environment-specific statistics, not random noise.

↔
Casino → CAM-LDS
Uninformative, not inverted

AUROC lands close to 0.50 in every model — the ranking simply carries no signal, rather than pointing backwards. Casino's narrower training set (4 techniques, 17% attack) doesn't contain enough variety to say anything useful about CAM‑LDS's broader, more diverse attack landscape.

06 · Root Cause

Why this keeps happening: how CAM‑LDS was built

Sections 03 and 04–05 describe two separate-looking failures. They share one root cause, found in the script that built the raw dataset — not in anything about the models.

CAM‑LDS's 54% attack rate is not a natural property of the monitored environment. It's the direct, deliberate output of the extraction script's sampling parameters:

# extract_caml_full_v3.py parser.add_argument('--benign_ratio', type=float, default=2.5) MAX_PER_SCENARIO_BENIGN = 15000 # also caps attack per technique # ↓ pushes all 19 techniques toward roughly equal representation, # and samples benign processes at ~2.5x the resulting attack count

This is a curated, class-balanced training benchmark — built so a supervised classifier has plenty of diverse, roughly-balanced examples to learn from. It was never meant to represent what attack prevalence looks like in a real deployment.

CAM-LDS attack rate 54.0% — engineered vs. Casino attack rate 17.1% — plausible real-world base rate
One construction choice — near-balanced sampling across all 19 techniques — produces two separate-looking downstream failures:
↓                        ↓
Consequence A · §03

Isolation Forest's structural failure isn't about "unusual" data — it's that the extraction script explicitly engineered away the sparse minority the algorithm needs. There was never a minority to isolate.

Consequence B · §04–05

Models learn to expect ~54% prevalence across 19 evenly-capped techniques. Casino looks nothing like that. The cross-dataset collapse is partly the well-documented phenomenon of models trained on a curated benchmark failing to generalize to realistic, imbalanced conditions — not purely a mysterious environmental difference.

This reframes an "unexplained distribution shift" into a specific, testable claim: regenerating CAM‑LDS at a realistic (~17%) attack rate and rerunning the pipeline should measurably improve both Isolation Forest's within-dataset score and the cross-dataset transfer — if the hypothesis is right. That experiment is queued but not yet run at the time of writing.

07 · Methodology, in brief
Thresholding: every model's decision cutoff is chosen to maximize F1 subject to a 5% FPR cap, calibrated on a held-out slice of training data only — never on test data.
Isolation Forest disclosure: "novelty mode" fits only on benign training rows. This uses labels to select the fit subset, making it semi-supervised rather than purely unsupervised — the trees themselves still never see y.
Leak-free CV: vector-group StratifiedGroupKFold ensures duplicate feature rows never span the train/test boundary, following the Milestone 2 methodology.
Cross-dataset features: restricted to the 17 features present in both corpora — the collapse above is not an artifact of missing features.
